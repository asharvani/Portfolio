Zepto · UX design challenge

Finding a mop shouldn't take two categories.

Reworking how Zepto groups and filters household products, so people find what they came for without endless scrolling.

Heuristic evaluation · Wireframes · Hi‑fi prototype · Usability testing · Dec 2023

In short

The problem

Near‑identical cleaning products were split between "Cleaning Aids" under Home Needs and "Cleaning Tools" under Cleaning Essentials, then dumped into one long scroll.

What I did

Ran a heuristic evaluation, sketched three directions, built a hi‑fi Figma prototype and tested it with quick‑commerce users.

The result

One merged subcategory, items grouped by type, plus sort, filter and quick filter pills — shaped further by what testing surfaced.

Why Zepto

I use Zepto regularly, so its friction was my friction. Picking an app from my own week meant I could spot the rough edges from lived experience, not just from screenshots.

The project came out of Anudeep Iyyagari's design challenge and workshop. His User Centred Design workbook pushed a plain approach: ask common‑sense questions about the experience, answer them honestly, then check each answer against established heuristics.

Supermarkets group things the way people think. The app didn't.

The problem

Browsing for household items, I kept hitting the same wall. Home Needs held a subcategory called Cleaning Aids. Cleaning Essentials held one called Cleaning Tools. The products inside both were largely the same kind of thing — and scattered across the screen in no particular order.

  1. Symptom

    Users scroll a long, unordered list hunting for one item.

  2. Because

    There's no sorting, filtering or grouping to narrow it down.

  3. Because

    Similar products are split across two subcategories in two parents.

  4. Root cause

    The catalogue follows internal structure, not the mental model of a shopper walking a supermarket aisle.

Four heuristics, broken

Mapping the common‑sense observations to Nielsen's heuristics made the gaps concrete.

01Match between system and the real world

Products weren't sorted the way people see them in a store, where items sit together by category.

02Flexibility and efficiency of use

Few ways to narrow down to a specific tool; users were stuck in a long list with no shortcuts.

03User control and freedom

An endless scroll was the only path, limiting how freely people could explore or jump to what they wanted.

04Recognition rather than recall

Comparing items meant remembering details, with little visual cueing to lean on.

Make the app shelve things the way shoppers already think.

Ideation

Three directions came out of brainstorming, each answering a different heuristic. They turned out to work best together.

Group within

Group items inside a subcategory by type, so mops sit with mops and brushes with brushes.

Sort & filter

Add sort and filter controls, plus quick filter pills along the top for one‑tap narrowing.

Merge the two

Collapse Cleaning Aids and Cleaning Tools into a single subcategory, so everything lives in one place.

first on paper

I sketched the screen on paper first: category rail on the left, pills across the top, Sort and Filter pinned at the bottom next to the delivery nudge. The strongest version moved into a high‑fidelity Figma prototype.

The redesign

What changed on the subcategory screen, and which heuristic each change answers.

Structure

One subcategory instead of two

Cleaning Aids and Cleaning Tools merge into a single home. Shoppers no longer need to guess which parent holds the item they want.

Navigation

Sort, filter and quick pills

Three pills — Toilet & other brushes, Jhaadu/Mops/Wipers, Brooms & Dust pans — sit above the grid. The selected pill shows a clear-cross, and the Filter sheet repeats the same categories alongside brand, price, discount and origin.

efficiency + control
Scanning

Grouped shelves you can recognise

Labelled groups break the endless list into short, predictable sections. Shoppers compare like with like on screen instead of holding details in their head.

Usability testing

I put the prototype in front of quick‑commerce users to check it worked, and to find what didn't. Each session followed the same five prompts.

Habits

How often do you use quick‑commerce apps, and which ones?

Finding items

Walk me through how you found the cleaning tools or home essentials you needed.

The merge

What do you think of Cleaning Aids and Cleaning Tools becoming one subcategory?

Friction

Any specific challenges or wins while navigating?

One change

If you could improve one thing based on this experience, what would it be?

The observations fed straight into another round of iterations before the final prototype.

Where the business feels it

This was a concept, not a shipped change, so these are the metrics I'd expect it to move rather than measured results.

Conv.↑

When people can find and filter quickly, more browsing sessions end in a purchase.

Bounce↓

Efficient navigation keeps people exploring instead of leaving a frustrating list.

CSAT↑

An easier shop builds satisfaction, loyalty and word of mouth.

What I'd take further

Questions I'd want answered before this reached production.

Does the merge scale?

Other categories likely have the same overlap. A catalogue‑wide audit would show how far this pattern should go.

Which pills earn their place?

Search and order data should decide the quick filters, not intuition.

Measure, don't assume

An A/B test on time‑to‑add‑to‑cart would turn the expected impact into real numbers.

Heuristics as a starting point

The workshop's common‑sense method found real issues fast; pairing it with analytics would sharpen prioritisation.
